Bio

My name is Takashi Fujiwara, and I am a doctoral student in the Department of AI Systems Medicine at the Institute of Biomedical Engineering, Institute of Science Tokyo.

My research in AI-driven drug discovery and bioinformatics draws on my training in mathematical and information sciences, the life sciences, and experimental methods.

I use deep learning and large language models (LLMs) to design molecules for drug discovery, including antibodies and peptides, and test them myself in the lab. By moving between computational design and experiments, I aim to develop new ways to identify drug candidates and contribute to science and technology in Japan.

Research

Molecular design

Antibody & peptide design

Starting with a target sequence, I design antibodies and prioritize candidates for synthesis. For peptides, I evaluate predicted binding and safety together.

View research
Multi-agent systems

AI agents for drug discovery

When one AI agent flags a problem with how a molecule might behave in the body, another revises its structure. I co-developed OrchestRA to put that feedback to work in drug design.

View research
Systems biology

Genomics of disease & regeneration

I compare gene expression in Parkinson’s and Huntington’s diseases to study changes in movement. Across species, I look for DNA patterns that humans share with an animal that can regrow a limb.

Pepti-drift: Scalable Safe-Active Peptide Generation Without Inference-Time Guidance

Takashi Fujiwara, Hikaru Shindo, Kaushalya Madhawa, Jun Jin Choong, Shuan Chen, Yuna Oikawa, Yiming Zhang, Gyubok Lee, Keisuke Ozawa

Longer peptides tended to score better for predicted binding and worse for safety, complicating comparisons between models. Across 88 held-out targets, 18.37% of Pepti-drift’s candidates met the predicted binding threshold and all four safety criteria.

Axolotl regeneration reveals a dormant cis-regulatory grammar conserved across vertebrate genomes

Takashi Fujiwara, Kazuki Nakanishi, Takahide Suzuki, Hideyuki Shimizu

We found combinations of short DNA motifs associated with axolotl limb regeneration near the corresponding genes in humans and mice. AlphaGenome predicted low transcriptional activity at the strongest mammalian candidate sites.
